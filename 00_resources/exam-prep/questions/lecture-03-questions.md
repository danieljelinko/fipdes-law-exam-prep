## Q1. Can plant-based products use terms like "milk", "sausage", "steak"?

**Topic area:** Misleading information (Topic 1) + Voluntary terms (Topic 8)
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Critically discuss whether plant-based products should be permitted to use traditionally meat- or dairy-derived terms such as "milk", "sausage", "steak", "cheese". Debate the issue from the perspective of the meat/dairy lobby and from the perspective of vegetarian/vegan advocates, citing the relevant EU rules on misleading information.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Frame as collision between two EU regulations:
   - **Reg (EU) No 1169/2011 Art 7** — food information shall not mislead.
   - **Reg (EU) No 1308/2013** — common organisation of markets in agricultural products — reserves "milk" and dairy terms to milk of mammalian origin (Annex VII Part III).
2. **Main body — meat/dairy lobby perspective:**
   - Consumer protection against confusion.
   - Plant-based products are not nutritionally equivalent — calling them "milk" suggests parity.
   - 1308/2013 already protects "milk", "cream", "butter", "cheese" — no use of dairy terms for non-mammalian products.
   - EU Court of Justice TofuTown ruling (2017) — confirmed dairy terms reserved.
3. **Main body — vegetarian/vegan perspective:**
   - Consumers understand "soya milk", "oat milk" by long usage.
   - Restricting plant-based terms hampers transition to sustainable diets (Farm to Fork Strategy).
   - 1308/2013 only restricts **dairy** terms; meat terms like "sausage", "burger", "steak" are **not currently reserved** under EU law.
   - Amendment 165 (rejected by EP 2020) would have restricted meat names.
4. **Conclusion:** Tension between consumer-protection rationale and sustainability/innovation. Argue whether labelling clarity (e.g. "plant-based burger") is sufficient, or whether reservation is needed.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 7 (fair information practices / misleading)
- Reg (EU) No 1308/2013, Annex VII Part III (dairy terms)
- ECJ Case C-422/16 *TofuTown* (2017)
- Farm to Fork Strategy (2020)

## Q2. Misleading information — Article 7 of 1169/2011

**Topic area:** Misleading information (Topic 1, Topic 6 cross-over)
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Outline the principal requirements of Article 7 of Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 concerning fair information practices. With reference to specific examples, discuss how food information may be considered misleading.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Cite Reg 1169/2011 Art 6 (basic requirement that all food for the final consumer be accompanied by food information) and Art 7 (the fair information practices).
2. **Main body — the four-fold Art 7.1 test:**
   - **(a)** as to characteristics — nature, identity, properties, composition, quantity, durability, country of origin, method of manufacture/production.
   - **(b)** by attributing effects/properties the food does not possess (e.g. "weight loss capabilities").
   - **(c)** by suggesting special characteristics when all similar foods possess them (e.g. "oranges containing Vitamin C" — all oranges do).
   - **(d)** by suggesting (via appearance, description, pictorial) the presence of a food/ingredient when in fact a normally-present component has been substituted (e.g. cheese substituted by analogue).
3. **Other Art 7 paragraphs:**
   - **Art 7.2** — info must be accurate, clear, easy to understand.
   - **Art 7.3** — no attribution of medicinal property (preventing/treating/curing disease).
   - **Art 7.4** — applies also to advertising and presentation.
4. **Examples:** Tesco "Everyday Value sweet & sour chicken" (slide 154); "with cheese" claim requiring QUID; "100% natural"/"farm fresh" — judged case-by-case.
5. **Conclusion:** Art 7 sits within a broader regulatory mesh (Art 8 of 178/2002, Reg 1924/2006 for claims). Enforcement remains uneven across MS.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 7.1(a)–(d), 7.2, 7.3, 7.4
- Reg (EC) No 178/2002, Article 8 (general consumer protection)
- Reg (EC) No 1924/2006 (nutrition and health claims)

## Q3. The 12 mandatory food information particulars

**Topic area:** FIC labelling — mandatory information (Topic 6)
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> List and explain the mandatory food information particulars required under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. For an NPD (new product development) product of your choice, demonstrate how each requirement would be satisfied on the label.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Cite Reg 1169/2011 Art 9 — mandatory particulars. List the 12 (slide 150):
   1. Name of the food (*)
   2. List of ingredients
   3. Allergens / intolerances (emphasised)
   4. QUID — Quantitative Ingredient Declaration
   5. Net quantity (*)
   6. Date of minimum durability or use-by date
   7. Special storage conditions / conditions of use
   8. Name/business name and address of FBO
   9. Country of origin
   10. Instructions for use where necessary
   11. Alcoholic strength for beverages > 1.2% ABV (*)
   12. Nutrition declaration
   *(* must appear in the same field of vision)*
2. **Main body — apply to chosen NPD product (e.g. ready-meal chicken curry):**
   - Name (legal or customary), ingredients list in descending order, **allergens bolded**, QUID for chicken (% in name), net quantity in metric, "use by" date if perishable, "keep refrigerated", FBO name + address, country of origin (incl. EU origin labelling for meat — Reg 1337/2013), reheat instructions, no alcohol ABV, mandatory nutrition table per 100 g.
3. **Format requirements:** font size ≥ 1.2 mm x-height (Art 13), legibility, no concealing/obscuring.
4. **Conclusion:** Compliance = checklist exercise; the lecturer's "knowledge and references missing" feedback signals that students must cite each particular by Art 9 and explain field-of-vision rule.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Articles 9 (mandatory), 13 (presentation), 14 (distance selling), 21 (allergens), 30 (nutrition declaration)
- Reg (EU) No 1337/2013 (origin labelling for meat — pigs, sheep, goats, poultry)
- FSAI eModules on labelling

## Q4. Allergen labelling under FIC

**Topic area:** Allergen Management (Topic 6)
**Difficulty:** explanatory + applied
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Discuss the requirements for allergen labelling under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. List the 14 allergens listed in Annex II and explain how they must be presented on (a) pre-packed and (b) non pre-packed foods.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Cite Reg 1169/2011 Art 21 + Annex II.
2. **Main body — the 14 allergens (Annex II):**
   1. Cereals containing gluten (wheat, rye, barley, oats, spelt, khorasan)
   2. Crustaceans
   3. Eggs
   4. Fish
   5. Peanuts
   6. Soybeans
   7. Milk (incl. lactose)
   8. Nuts (almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, cashews, pecans, brazils, pistachios, macadamia)
   9. Celery
   10. Mustard
   11. Sesame seeds
   12. Sulphur dioxide & sulphites (> 10 mg/kg or 10 mg/L SO₂)
   13. Lupin
   14. Molluscs
3. **Presentation rules:**
   - **Pre-packed:** declared in ingredients list AND **emphasised** (bold, italic, contrasting font/colour/background) — Reg 1169/2011 Art 21.
   - "Contains" boxes **no longer allowed** — allergens highlighted within ingredients list only.
   - **Non pre-packed** (since 13 Dec 2014): allergens must also be declared (restaurants, delis, takeaways).
   - For cereals containing gluten: highlight the **cereal name** (e.g. wheat, oats), not the word "gluten".
   - For nuts: highlight the **actual nut name** (hazelnut), not the word "nut".
4. **Pine nuts and coconuts** are NOT in Annex II nuts category (slide 176).
5. **Conclusion:** Allergen labelling is one of the most frequent enforcement issues; FSAI prioritises this. Reg 2021/382 extended food safety culture obligations to allergen management.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 21, Annex II (14 allergens)
- Reg (EU) 2021/382 (food safety culture incl. allergen management)
- FSAI guidance on allergens

## Q5. QUID — Quantitative Ingredient Declaration

**Topic area:** FIC — QUID & nutrition declaration (Topic 6)
**Difficulty:** applied calculation + explanatory
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Explain the QUID rules under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. When is QUID required, when is it exempt, and how is it calculated? Illustrate with a worked example.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Cite Reg 1169/2011 Art 22 + Annex VIII. QUID = the percentage of an ingredient, declared on the label.
2. **Main body — QUID is required when:**
   - The ingredient is **included in the name of the food** (e.g. cheese & onion quiche → cheese AND onion must be quantified).
   - The ingredient is **associated** with the name (e.g. "summer pudding" → fruit content).
   - The ingredient is **emphasised** in words, pictures or graphics (e.g. "with cheese" → cheese %).
3. **QUID is not required when** (5 conditions, slide 180):
   - Solid in liquid medium (kidney beans in salt water) — drained net weight already declared.
   - Existing legislation already mandates it (e.g. fruit nectar fruit content under Directive 2001/112/EC).
   - Small quantity (≤ 2%) used for flavouring (garlic in garlic bread).
   - Ingredient in name but in such small quantity as not to affect purchasing decisions (malt in malt whiskey).
   - No ingredient significantly predominates (mixture of fruit/vegetables).
4. **Calculation (slide 182, Butter Cookie example):**
   - Mixing-bowl weight: Flour 100, Sugar 35, Butter 50, Eggs 10 → Total 195 g.
   - After baking: 169 g.
   - QUID = 50 / 169 × 100 = **30 %** (use **finished product** weight as denominator, not mixing-bowl).
5. **Conclusion:** QUID rules are mechanical but easy to fail — the lecturer's chilli con carne exercise (slide 183) tests exactly this.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 22, Annex VIII
- Directive 2001/112/EC on fruit juices
- Worked example: mixing-bowl vs finished-product calculation

## Q6. Date marking — best before vs use by

**Topic area:** FIC — date marking (Topic 6)
**Difficulty:** explanatory + applied
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Distinguish between "best before" and "use by" date marking under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. Discuss the link between date marking and food waste, and outline the changes introduced regarding ice cream, large beverage containers, and frozen meat.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Cite Reg 1169/2011 Art 24 — date of minimum durability ("best before") or "use by" for highly perishable foods.
2. **Main body — distinction:**
   - **Best before** — date until which food retains specific properties (taste, aroma, appearance, vitamin content) when stored correctly. Used for canned, dried, ambient, frozen foods. Safe to eat after, but quality may have deteriorated.
   - **Use by** — used for foods that are highly perishable from a microbiological point of view (likely to constitute immediate danger to human health after a short period). E.g. raw milk, minced meat, fish, ready-to-eat salads.
   - **'Use by' expired = food unsafe** under Art 14 of Reg 178/2002.
3. **Format requirements (Art 24 + Annex X):**
   - Use by: day, month, optionally year.
   - Best before: short shelf life (< 3 months) → day, month; 3–18 months → end month + year; > 18 months → end year.
4. **Exemptions (Annex X):** fresh fruit/veg, wines, beverages > 10% alcohol, bakery products consumed within 24 h, vinegar, cooking salt, solid sugar, chewing gum.
5. **Changes / extensions:**
   - Individual portions of ice cream — **no longer exempt**.
   - Soft drinks, fruit juices, alcoholic beverages > 5 L — **no longer exempt**.
   - Frozen meat, frozen meat preparations, frozen unprocessed fishery products — must show date of freezing or "frozen on…" if frozen more than once.
6. **Food waste link:** Confusion between "best before" and "use by" drives consumer waste. EU Commission Market Study on date marking (2018) — proposed revisions under Farm to Fork.
7. **Conclusion:** Date marking is a regulatory tool serving safety, quality, and waste-prevention objectives — sometimes in tension.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Articles 24, 25, Annex X
- Reg (EC) No 178/2002, Article 14 (use-by + unsafe = automatically unsafe)
- EFSA Guidance on date marking (Part 1 — date marking; Part 2 — food information)
- EU Commission Market Study on date marking and food waste prevention (2018, DOI: 10.2875/808514)

## Q7. The objective of food information — informed choice

**Topic area:** FIC — overarching principles (Topic 1, Topic 6)
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> "The fundamental objective of food information is to enable consumers to make informed choices." Critically discuss this statement with reference to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. To what extent does the regulation actually achieve this objective?

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Cite the overarching aim — Reg 1169/2011 Art 3.1: *"high level of protection of consumers' health and interests by providing a basis for final consumers to make informed choices"*.
2. **Main body — supporting:**
   - Mandatory particulars (Art 9) ensure baseline information.
   - Allergen emphasis (Art 21) supports informed choice for allergic consumers.
   - Nutrition declaration (Art 30) supports informed dietary choices.
   - Article 7 prevents misleading information.
3. **Main body — limits:**
   - **Information overload** — small font, dense panels; the "informed consumer" is an idealisation.
   - **Front-of-pack (FoP)** schemes inconsistent across MS — Nutri-Score (France/Belgium) vs traffic light (UK) vs reference intakes (industry-preferred).
   - **Health-literacy gradient** — same label doesn't serve all consumers equally.
   - **Greenwashing** and **voluntary claims** not fully addressed in FIC.
4. **Future direction:** EU's Inception Impact Assessment (slide 206) — proposed FIC revision: harmonised mandatory front-of-pack nutrition labelling, nutrient profiles for HFSS foods, extension of origin labelling, revision of date marking.
5. **Conclusion:** FIC sets the baseline but is increasingly seen as insufficient to meet contemporary consumer-protection goals (especially nutrition and sustainability).

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Articles 3, 7, 9, 21, 22, 24, 30
- EU FIC revision Inception Impact Assessment
- FSAI Survey: Irish attitudes to food labelling (2009)
- Nutri-Score; UK traffic-light system

## Q8. Greenwashing and sustainability claims on food labels

**Topic area:** Misleading information + Voluntary/sustainability claims (Topics 6 + 8)
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Critically discuss how voluntary sustainability claims (e.g. "eco-friendly", "carbon-neutral", "sustainable", "ethically sourced") on food labels should be regulated to prevent greenwashing. Refer to the current rules under FIC and any proposed reforms.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Define greenwashing — unsubstantiated or misleading environmental claims. Currently NOT specifically regulated under FIC; falls under Art 7 misleading-information umbrella.
2. **Main body — current EU position:**
   - Reg (EU) No 1169/2011 Art 7 catches misleading **environmental** claims indirectly.
   - Reg (EU) No 2018/848 — organic labelling tightly controlled (certified, EU logo, specific rules).
   - No equivalent for general sustainability terms.
   - Farm to Fork Strategy (2020) — proposed harmonised framework for sustainable food.
3. **Examples of problem areas:**
   - "Country of Origin" usage outside mandatory categories — sometimes used to imply local sustainability.
   - Voluntary "free-from" claims (free-from meat, plant-based, reduced meat).
   - Carbon labelling — voluntary, methodologies vary.
4. **Proposals (EU level):**
   - EU Green Claims Directive (proposed 2023) — pre-approval of voluntary environmental claims.
   - Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive (2024) — bans generic claims like "environmentally friendly" without substantiation.
5. **Conclusion:** Without specific positive rules, sustainability claims sit in a grey zone — Art 7 prohibits misleading but doesn't define what counts as substantiated. Reform direction = pre-substantiation + standardised methodologies.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Reg (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 7
- Reg (EU) 2018/848 (organic production and labelling)
- Reg (EU) No 1308/2013 (marketing of agricultural products — voluntary terms like PDO, PGI, TSG)
- EU Farm to Fork Strategy (2020); EU Green Claims Directive (proposed)
- Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive (2024)
