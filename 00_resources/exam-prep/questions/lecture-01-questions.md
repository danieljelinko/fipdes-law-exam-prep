## Q1. The need for food regulation

**Topic area:** General Food Law — foundations
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Discuss the historical drivers behind the development of food regulation in Europe. Why is regulation necessary in the food industry, and what role did major food crises play in shaping the current legal framework?

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Define what food regulation aims to achieve — consumer protection, fair trade, public health. Reference Reg (EC) 178/2002 as the consolidating instrument.
2. **Main body:**
   - Pre-2002 fragmented national rules and free-movement tensions.
   - Need for harmonisation under EU single market.
   - Role of Codex Alimentarius (international standards) and WTO/SPS Agreement.
   - Failures in pre-178/2002 system: BSE crisis, dioxin crisis.
3. **Examples:** BSE (UK 1986, Ireland 1989) and Dioxin (Belgium 1999) — concrete triggers for the Green Paper (1997) → White Paper (2000) → Reg 178/2002 cascade.
4. **Conclusion:** Evaluate whether the current framework adequately addresses the historical failures it was designed to fix.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Codex Alimentarius (FAO/WHO joint body)
- Green Paper on Food Law, May 1997
- White Paper on Food Safety, January 2000
- Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 — laying down general principles and requirements of food law

## Q2. The EU institutions and food law-making

**Topic area:** General Food Law — institutional framework
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Describe the role of the main EU institutions — the Commission, Parliament and Council — in the development and adoption of food law. How does an EU regulation differ from an EU directive, and why does this distinction matter for food businesses?

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Brief overview of EU as supranational legal order; food law principally adopted under ordinary legislative procedure.
2. **Main body:**
   - **Commission** — proposes legislation, monitors implementation (DG SANTE), runs RASFF.
   - **Parliament** — co-legislator; democratically elected.
   - **Council** — co-legislator (Member States).
   - **Regulation vs Directive distinction:**
     - Regulation = directly applicable, identical effect in all MS (e.g. 178/2002, 1169/2011).
     - Directive = sets goal, MS choose implementation (e.g. Directive 2011/91/EU on batch/lot indications).
3. **Examples:** Reg 178/2002 (regulation, direct effect) vs the older food-labelling directives that 1169/2011 repealed.
4. **Conclusion:** For food businesses, regulations create a level playing field; directives leave room for national variation — affects compliance burden across MS.

### Key references to score extra marks
- DG SANTE (Directorate-General for Health and Food Safety)
- TFEU Article 168 (public health) and Article 169 (consumer protection)
- Directive 2011/91/EU — batch/lot marking

## Q3. Codex Alimentarius and international food standards

**Topic area:** General Food Law — international context
**Difficulty:** extension (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> What is Codex Alimentarius, and what role does it play in shaping European food legislation? Discuss the relationship between Codex, the WTO SPS Agreement, and EU food law.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Codex = joint FAO/WHO body, established 1963, sets voluntary international food standards.
2. **Main body:**
   - Codex standards are voluntary but referenced by WTO SPS Agreement as the international benchmark for food safety.
   - WTO SPS Agreement — Members must base SPS measures on scientific evidence and international standards (Codex) or justify divergence.
   - EU often uses Codex as the floor but applies stricter rules (e.g. growth hormones in beef, GMO).
3. **Examples:** GMO labelling (EU stricter than Codex/US); precautionary principle as justification for divergence.
4. **Conclusion:** EU treats Codex as a foundation, not a ceiling — the precautionary principle (Reg 178/2002 Art 7) allows for higher EU standards.

### Key references to score extra marks
- WTO SPS Agreement (1995)
- Codex Alimentarius Commission
- Reg (EC) No 178/2002, Article 7 (precautionary principle)
- Bevilacqua (2015) on global food safety law principles

## Q4. Food scares as drivers of law reform

**Topic area:** Risk analysis & crisis-driven legislation (overlap with Topic 1)
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> "The most significant developments in EU food law have been reactions to crises, not proactive design." Critically discuss this statement with reference to at least three named food-safety incidents.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** State the thesis — much of EU food law is post-crisis. Cite Reg 178/2002 as direct response to BSE + dioxin.
2. **Main body — three crises:**
   - **BSE (1986–1996):** UK/Ireland cattle culling, link to vCJD, MBM ban, Southwood Report failure, BSE Inquiry Report 2000 highlighted **risk communication failure** → drove EFSA's "independence" mandate.
   - **Dioxin (Belgium 1999):** PCB-contaminated feed → Green Paper (1997) → White Paper (2000) → Reg 178/2002.
   - **Horsemeat scandal (2013):** drove tightening of food labelling rules and contributed to FIC 1169/2011 enforcement emphasis.
   - Optional: E. coli (Fenugreek seeds 2011), Sudan 1, Olive oil fraud.
3. **Counter-argument:** Some proactive elements — precautionary principle (Art 7), HACCP harmonisation under 852/2004, Farm to Fork Strategy.
4. **Conclusion:** Pattern is mostly reactive; argue whether this is necessarily a flaw (responsiveness) or a problem (legislative lag).

### Key references to score extra marks
- Southwood Report (1989); BSE Inquiry Report (2001)
- Reg (EC) No 178/2002 of 28 January 2002
- Knowles & McEachern (2007) "European Food Scares and their impact on EU food policy"
- Farm to Fork Strategy (Green New Deal)
