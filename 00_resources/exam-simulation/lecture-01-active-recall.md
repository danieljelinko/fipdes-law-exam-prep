### Codex Alimentarius — the *Food Code*

The Codex Alimentarius, or "Food Code", is a collection of standards, guidelines and codes of practice adopted by the Codex Alimentarius Commission (CAC). The Commission is the central part of the Joint FAO/WHO Food Standards Programme and was established by FAO and WHO to protect consumer health and promote fair practices in food trade. It held its first meeting in **1963**.

Codex standards ensure that food is safe and can be traded — helping to remove barriers to trade.

**187 Codex members** have negotiated science-based recommendations in all areas related to food safety and quality:

- Food hygiene
- Maximum limits for food additives
- Residues of pesticides and veterinary drugs
- Maximum limits and codes for the prevention of chemical and microbiological contamination

### General Principles governing Global Food Safety Law

*Source: Bevilacqua (2015)*

- **Risk Analysis** — *"… a systematic way of gathering, evaluating and recording information which would lead to recommendations, positions or actions in response to an identified hazard."* (WTO, 2000). Four components: risk policy, risk assessment, risk management, risk communication. See SPS Agreement and CAC Procedural Manual.

- **Precautionary Principle** — Enables decision-makers to adopt precautionary measures when scientific evidence about an environmental or human-health hazard is uncertain and the stakes are high. Not formally defined as such, but its requirements are laid out in **Art 7 of Reg (EC) 178/2002**.

- **Principle of Proportionality** — The rule of law is fluid; decision-making should be coherent with the purposes, adequate to reach its tasks, rationally motivated, and directed to cause the least harm to conflicting interests.

- **Principle of Reasonableness** — Adequacy between the legislative prescriptions and the administrative decision.

### The Need for a European Policy

EU Food law developed **incrementally** over a long period of time.

- The **Treaty of Rome** made no explicit mention of consumer protection or public health.
- Not until the **Single European Act (1986)** and the **Maastricht Treaty (1992)** were consumer protection and public health introduced as EU goals.
- Food law concentrated on the **free movement of foodstuffs** in the earlier years.

Key milestones:

- **1962** — The first EU food Directive, concerned with colours in foodstuffs.
- **1979** — *Cassis de Dijon* and other cases pushed the EU to reorientate its food law.

## Slide 24

![Slide 24](food_law_slides/slide_024.png)

### EU: Free Movement of Goods — *Principle of Mutual Recognition (Cassis de Dijon Case)*

> Once a food product is legally marketable in one member State, it must (subject to certain exceptions) be legally marketable in all EU member States.

**Repercussions:**

- Harmonisation
- Generic foods / foodstuffs
- Low common denominator (quality)
- Locally and artisan production methods come under threat
- Larger manufacturers are in a stronger position to lobby
- Foods from outside the EU — WTO and International Concerns
- Food Nutrition — Animal Welfare — Human Rights

*See the Cassis de Dijon case for the foundational ruling.*

### What is EU Law?

EU law is divided into **primary** and **secondary** legislation:

1. **Primary Legislation** — The **treaties** are the basis or ground rules for all EU action.
2. **Secondary Legislation** — Derived from the principles and objectives set out in the treaties; includes:
    1. Regulations
    2. Directives
    3. Decisions
    4. Recommendations
    5. Opinions

Member States have primary responsibility for the correct and timely application of EU Treaties and legislation. The Commission monitors the application of Union law and may take action if a Member State fails to introduce directives or is suspected of breaches.

## Slide 29

### The Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU)

The **Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU)** is one of two treaties forming the constitutional basis of the EU, the other being the **Treaty on European Union (TEU)** — also referred to as the *Treaty of Maastricht*.

The Treaty originated as the **Treaty of Rome** (full name: *Treaty establishing the European Economic Community*), which brought about the creation of the European Economic Community (EEC). It was signed on **25 March 1957** by six countries — Belgium, France, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and West Germany — and came into force on **1 January 1958**.

Its name has been amended **twice** since 1957:

- The **Maastricht Treaty (1992)** removed the word "economic" from the Treaty of Rome's official title.
- In **2009**, the **Treaty of Lisbon** renamed it the *Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union*.

In 2005, a referendum was held to attempt to launch a European Constitution. This failed, and on **13 December 2007** the Lisbon treaty was signed — this saw the 'TEC' renamed as the TFEU and renumbered.

### How are decisions made in the EU?

The EU's standard decision-making procedure is known as the **Ordinary Legislative Procedure** (formerly *"codecision"*):

1. The **European Commission** drafts and implements EU legislation.
2. Both the **European Parliament** (directly elected) and the **EU Council** (28 governments) must approve EU legislation.

*Reference:* <http://www.europarl.europa.eu/aboutparliament/en/20150201PVL00004/Legislative-powers>

The procedure flows through these stages:

1. **Commission** drafts a **Proposal**

## Slide 33

![Slide 33](food_law_slides/slide_033.png)

### Voting for EU Law

The EU has **3 legislative (law-making) institutions**:

1. The **European Commission** proposes new laws.
2. These proposals must be passed by both the **European Parliament** and …
3. … the **Council of the European Union**.

#### Qualified Majority Voting (QMV)

In order for a decision to be passed by the Council:

- **At least 55 %** of member states must agree, **and**
- the member states in agreement must represent at least **65 %** of the EU population.
- To **block** a proposal, at least **4 member states** representing at least **35 %** of the EU population must vote against it.

## Slide 35

![Slide 35](food_law_slides/slide_035.png)

### Secondary Legislation — *Binding*

- **Regulations** — A regulation is a **binding** legislative act. It must be applied in its **entirety** across the EU.

- **Directives** — A directive is a legislative act that sets out a **goal** that all EU countries must achieve. However, it is up to the individual countries to devise their own laws on **how** to reach these goals.

- **Decisions** — A decision is **binding on those to whom it is addressed** (e.g. an EU country or an individual company) and is directly applicable.

## Slide 36

![Slide 36](food_law_slides/slide_036.png)

### Secondary Legislation — *Non-Binding*

- **Recommendations** — A recommendation is **not binding**.

- **Opinions** — An opinion is an instrument that allows the institutions to make a statement in a non-binding fashion (without imposing any legal obligation on those to whom it is addressed). An opinion is **not binding**.

#### But what can non-binding secondary legislation achieve?

- Allow EU institutions to make their views known and suggest a course of action.
- Influence policy.
- Some recommendations and opinions may later feed into mandatory rules.

### EU Law and Irish Law

> European Union legislation is **superior** to all national legislation — European law **takes precedence** over national law.

National infrastructure to cope with the influx of EU Directives, Regulations and Decisions:

- **New Statutes** — laws enacted by the House of Oireachtas pursuant to **Constitution Article 15.2.1**.
- **Statutory Instruments** — a subordinate form of law that can be brought into power by Government (also pursuant to **Constitution Article 15.2.2**).

## Q1. The need for food regulation

**Topic area:** General Food Law — foundations
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Discuss the historical drivers behind the development of food regulation in Europe. Why is regulation necessary in the food industry, and what role did major food crises play in shaping the current legal framework?

### Answer outline

## Q2. The EU institutions and food law-making

**Topic area:** General Food Law — institutional framework
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Describe the role of the main EU institutions — the Commission, Parliament and Council — in the development and adoption of food law. How does an EU regulation differ from an EU directive, and why does this distinction matter for food businesses?

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Brief overview of EU as supranational legal order; food law principally adopted under ordinary legislative procedure.
2. **Main body:**
   - **Commission** — proposes legislation, monitors implementation (DG SANTE), runs RASFF.
   - **Parliament** — co-legislator; democratically elected.
   - **Council** — co-legislator (Member States).
   - **Regulation vs Directive distinction:**
     - Regulation = directly applicable, identical effect in all MS (e.g. 178/2002, 1169/2011).
     - Directive = sets goal, MS choose implementation (e.g. Directive 2011/91/EU on batch/lot indications).
3. **Examples:** Reg 178/2002 (regulation, direct effect) vs the older food-labelling directives that 1169/2011 repealed.
4. **Conclusion:** For food businesses, regulations create a level playing field; directives leave room for national variation — affects compliance burden across MS.

### Key references to score extra marks
- DG SANTE (Directorate-General for Health and Food Safety)
- TFEU Article 168 (public health) and Article 169 (consumer protection)

## Q3. Codex Alimentarius and international food standards

**Topic area:** General Food Law — international context
**Difficulty:** extension (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> What is Codex Alimentarius, and what role does it play in shaping European food legislation? Discuss the relationship between Codex, the WTO SPS Agreement, and EU food law.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Codex = joint FAO/WHO body, established 1963, sets voluntary international food standards.
2. **Main body:**
   - Codex standards are voluntary but referenced by WTO SPS Agreement as the international benchmark for food safety.
   - WTO SPS Agreement — Members must base SPS measures on scientific evidence and international standards (Codex) or justify divergence.
   - EU often uses Codex as the floor but applies stricter rules (e.g. growth hormones in beef, GMO).
3. **Examples:** GMO labelling (EU stricter than Codex/US); precautionary principle as justification for divergence.
4. **Conclusion:** EU treats Codex as a foundation, not a ceiling — the precautionary principle (Reg 178/2002 Art 7) allows for higher EU standards.

### Key references to score extra marks
- WTO SPS Agreement (1995)
- Codex Alimentarius Commission
- Reg (EC) No 178/2002, Article 7 (precautionary principle)
- Bevilacqua (2015) on global food safety law principles

## Q4. Food scares as drivers of law reform

**Topic area:** Risk analysis & crisis-driven legislation (overlap with Topic 1)
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> "The most significant developments in EU food law have been reactions to crises, not proactive design." Critically discuss this statement with reference to at least three named food-safety incidents.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** State the thesis — much of EU food law is post-crisis. Cite Reg 178/2002 as direct response to BSE + dioxin.
2. **Main body — three crises:**
   - **BSE (1986–1996):** UK/Ireland cattle culling, link to vCJD, MBM ban, Southwood Report failure, BSE Inquiry Report 2000 highlighted **risk communication failure** → drove EFSA's "independence" mandate.
   - **Dioxin (Belgium 1999):** PCB-contaminated feed → Green Paper (1997) → White Paper (2000) → Reg 178/2002.
   - **Horsemeat scandal (2013):** drove tightening of food labelling rules and contributed to FIC 1169/2011 enforcement emphasis.
   - Optional: E. coli (Fenugreek seeds 2011), Sudan 1, Olive oil fraud.
3. **Counter-argument:** Some proactive elements — precautionary principle (Art 7), HACCP harmonisation under 852/2004, Farm to Fork Strategy.
4. **Conclusion:** Pattern is mostly reactive; argue whether this is necessarily a flaw (responsiveness) or a problem (legislative lag).

### Key references to score extra marks
- Southwood Report (1989); BSE Inquiry Report (2001)
- Reg (EC) No 178/2002 of 28 January 2002
- Knowles & McEachern (2007) "European Food Scares and their impact on EU food policy"
- Farm to Fork Strategy (Green New Deal)
