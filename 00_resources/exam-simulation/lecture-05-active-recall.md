### Regulation (EC) No. 1169/2011 — Articles 6 and 7

**Basic requirement (Article 6):**
Any food intended for supply to the final consumer or to mass caterers shall be accompanied by food information in accordance with this Regulation.

**Fair information practices (Article 7):**

1. Food information shall **not be misleading**, particularly:
    - a. as to the characteristics of the food and, in particular, as to its nature, identity, properties, composition, quantity, durability, country of origin or place of provenance, method of manufacture or production
    - b. by attributing to the food effects or properties which it does not possess
    - c. by suggesting that the food possesses special characteristics when in fact all similar foods possess such characteristics, in particular by specifically emphasising the presence or absence of certain ingredients and/or nutrients
    - d. by suggesting, by means of the appearance, the description or pictorial representations, the presence of a particular food or an ingredient, while in reality a component naturally present or an ingredient normally used in that food has been substituted with a different component or a different ingredient
2. Food information shall be **accurate, clear and easy to understand** for the consumer.
3. Subject to derogations provided for by Union law, food information shall **not attribute to any food the property of preventing, treating or curing a human disease**.
4. Paragraphs 1, 2 and 3 shall also apply to: a. advertising; b. the presentation of foods, in particular their shape, appearance or packaging, the packaging materials used, the way in which they are arranged and the setting in which they are displayed.

### Fairtrade

Fairtrade is an alternative approach to conventional trade and is based on a
partnership between some of the most disadvantaged farmers and workers
in the developing world and the people who buy their products.

WHAT DOES THE FAIRTRADE MARK MEAN?
When a product carries the Fairtrade Mark it means it was produced
according to international Fairtrade standards. These standards ensure
Fairtrade products are socially and economically fair and environmentally
responsible. Key standards include the payment of a minimum price and a
premium.

## Slide 315

![Slide 315](food_law_slides/slide_315.png)

### THE FAIRTRADE MINIMUM PRICE

For most Fairtrade goods there is a Fairtrade minimum price which is
set to cover the cost of sustainable production for that product in that
region. If the market price for that product is higher than our minimum
price, then farmers and workers receive the market price.

### Using gluten-free on your label

- The use of the ‘gluten-free’ declaration is voluntary and can only be used where the food as sold to the final consumer (either prepacked
- r non-prepacked) contains no more than 20 mg/kg of gluten.

- The use of the ‘gluten-free’ declaration should not mislead

consumers, in line with the requirements of Article 7 of the legislation
- n the provision of food information to consumers (Regulation (EU) No. 1169/2011). For example, stating that a jam is gluten-free is misleading to the consumer, as it implies that this jam is special when, in fact, all jams are gluten-free.

### What are Plant Protection Products?

Plant protection products are **'pesticides' that protect crops or desirable or useful plants**.

They are primarily used in the agricultural sector but also in forestry, horticulture, amenity areas and in home gardens.

They contain at least one active substance and have one of the following functions:

- Protect plants or plant products against pests/diseases, before or after harvest
- Influence the life processes of plants (such as substances influencing their growth, excluding nutrients)
- Preserve plant products
- Destroy or prevent growth of undesired plants or parts of plants

They may also contain other components including safeners and synergists. Glyphosate is the active substance in Roundup.

### Some facts about glyphosate plants

- Glyphosate is an active substance used in plant protection products to

control plants, which means it's a herbicide

- Glyphosate is the most frequently used herbicide both worldwide and in

the EU and it has been used for several decades

- Glyphosate has been thoroughly assessed by Member States, the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) in recent years

- Glyphosate-based pesticides are used as herbicides in agriculture,

horticulture and in some non-cultivated areas

- They are used primarily to combat weeds that compete with cultivated crops or present problems for other reasons (e.g. on railway tracks)

- They are typically applied before crops are sown to control weeds and therefore facilitate better growth of crops by eliminating competing

- This eliminates or minimises the need to use ploughing machines ("zero tillage" farming), thereby reducing soil erosion and carbon emissions

- Glyphosate is also used to a lesser extent as a pre-harvest treatment to facilitate better harvesting by regulating plant growth and ripening

Source:  https://ec.europa.eu/food/plant/pesticides/glyphosate_en

### US Cases — Roundup / Glyphosate

- A lawyer for a man who said his cancer was caused by Bayer AG's glyphosate-based weed killer Roundup urged U.S. jurors to "send a message" to the company by holding it liable and awarding millions in damages. The case was the second of more than 11,200 Roundup lawsuits. "A responsible company would test its product. A responsible company would tell their customers if they knew it causes cancer," said the plaintiff's lawyer. She called conduct by Bayer's Monsanto unit reckless and offensive.
- Bayer, which bought Roundup maker Monsanto in a $63 billion deal, denies the allegations, saying decades of studies by independent scientists have shown glyphosate and Roundup to be safe for human use. In Hardeman's case, the jury found Roundup to have been a "substantial factor" in causing his non-Hodgkin's lymphoma.
- The decision allowed the trial to proceed to a second phase in which the same jury would decide if Bayer is liable. In the second phase lawyers for Hardeman were able to present previously excluded internal documents allegedly showing the company's efforts to influence scientists and regulators about the popular product's safety. Jurors would decide whether Roundup was defectively designed, whether Monsanto acted negligently, and if it failed to warn consumers of Roundup's cancer risks.
- Bayer's lawyer said the U.S. EPA, which regulates herbicides, never removed Roundup from the market or required a cancer warning. He also said any contribution to scientific studies by Monsanto employees had been properly disclosed, accusing plaintiffs of taking cherry-picked emails out of context.
- The EPA, the European Chemicals Agency and other regulators have found that glyphosate is **not likely carcinogenic** to humans. The World Health Organization's cancer arm, however, reached a different conclusion in 2015, classifying glyphosate as **"probably carcinogenic to humans."**
- In the first U.S. Roundup trial, another California man was awarded $289 million after a state court jury found the weed killer caused his cancer. That award was later reduced to $78 million and is on appeal.

## Q1. Voluntary food information and misleading claims

**Topic area:** Voluntary terms
**Difficulty:** core / debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Discuss how EU food law regulates voluntary food information. Use vegetarian/vegan, gluten-free, organic and Fairtrade examples to explain the difference between legally defined claims, private certification and general misleading-information controls.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Voluntary information is allowed, but it must not mislead. Anchor the answer in Reg (EU) No 1169/2011 Articles 7 and 36.
2. **Main body:**
   - **Vegetarian/vegan:** no EU legal definition. Article 36(3)(b) of Reg 1169/2011 anticipated EU rules, but none are in force. ISO 23662:2021 and schemes such as V-Label or Vegan Society Trademark are voluntary/private.
   - **Gluten-free:** legally controlled by Reg (EU) No 828/2014. "Gluten-free" means not more than 20 mg/kg; "very low gluten" means above 20 and not more than 100 mg/kg.
   - **Organic:** legally controlled by Reg (EU) 2018/848. "Organic", "bio" and "eco" are protected terms; operators must be certified and use the EU organic logo where required.
   - **Fairtrade:** voluntary certification, not a general EU food-law category; based on certification rules such as minimum price and premium.
   - Article 7 still applies to all claims, including claims that imply special characteristics shared by all similar foods.
3. **Application:** A jam labelled "gluten-free" can be misleading because ordinary jam is naturally gluten-free; the claim suggests a special characteristic that comparable jams also possess.
4. **Conclusion:** Voluntary claims range from fully regulated legal categories to private marks, but all sit under the baseline requirement that information be accurate, clear and not misleading.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Articles 7 and 36(3)(b)
- Regulation (EU) No 828/2014
- Regulation (EU) 2018/848
- ISO 23662:2021
- FSAI Guidance Document 29 (marketing terms)

## Q2. Organic, geographical indications and GMO labelling

**Topic area:** Specific voluntary / regulated terms
**Difficulty:** core (explanatory)
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Compare the legal treatment of organic claims, protected geographical indications and GMO labelling in EU food law. Why are these terms more tightly controlled than ordinary marketing language?

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** These are not ordinary promotional phrases; they are protected legal categories designed to prevent consumer deception and preserve market integrity.
2. **Main body:**
   - **Organic:** Reg (EU) 2018/848 governs production and labelling. Operators in Ireland are inspected by Organic Certification Bodies regulated by DAFM. The EU organic logo is mandatory for compliant prepacked organic food.
   - **PDO/PGI/TSG:** Reg (EU) No 1151/2012 protects quality schemes.
   - **PDO:** strongest geographical link; production, processing and preparation occur in the defined area.
   - **PGI:** at least one production step occurs in the area and the product has a quality, reputation or characteristic attributable to that area.
   - **TSG:** protects traditional composition or production process, not necessarily a geographical origin; at least 25 years' domestic use was referenced in the lecture.
   - **GMO:** Reg (EC) No 1829/2003 and Reg (EC) No 1830/2003 require labelling where food contains, consists of or is produced from GMOs. Wording such as "genetically modified" or "produced from genetically modified [ingredient]" must appear in the ingredients list.
3. **Application:** Irish examples include Imokilly Regato (PDO), Connemara Hill Lamb (PGI), Waterford Blaa/Blaa (PGI) and Irish Grass Fed Beef (PGI).
4. **Conclusion:** These schemes protect consumers, producers and the single market by preventing free-riding on origin, production method or genetic status.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Regulation (EU) 2018/848
- Regulation (EU) No 1151/2012
- Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003
- Regulation (EC) No 1830/2003
- S.I. No. 296 of 2015

## Q3. Food fraud and greenwashing

**Topic area:** Food fraud and sustainability claims
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Critically discuss the regulation of food fraud and greenwashing in EU food law. In your answer, consider whether existing rules on misleading information are sufficient to address sustainability and authenticity claims.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Frame fraud and greenwashing as consumer-protection issues under Reg 178/2002 Article 8 and Reg 1169/2011 Article 7.
2. **Main body - food fraud:**
   - Food fraud includes intentional substitution, dilution, adulteration or misrepresentation for economic gain.
   - Horsemeat scandal (2013) is the central example: beef products contained undeclared horsemeat, exposing supply-chain authenticity gaps.
   - Sudan 1 dye and balsamic vinegar fraud show that fraud can also involve illegal additives or false origin/composition.
   - Traceability and RASFF help response, but fraud may not always present as an immediate safety hazard.
3. **Main body - greenwashing:**
   - Sustainability claims are often voluntary and can mislead if vague, unverifiable or implying environmental superiority without evidence.
   - Article 7 prohibits misleading claims as to characteristics and production method.
   - Article 36 requires voluntary information not to mislead, not be ambiguous or confusing, and where appropriate be based on relevant scientific data.
   - Farm to Fork Strategy points toward stronger sustainability labelling, but the exam answer should recognise that the current framework is still fragmented.
4. **Conclusion:** Existing misleading-information rules are essential but not fully sufficient. Fraud and greenwashing require verification, traceability, enforcement and clearer harmonised standards for sustainability claims.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, Article 8
- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Articles 7 and 36
- RASFF
- Farm to Fork Strategy
- Horsemeat scandal (2013); Sudan 1
