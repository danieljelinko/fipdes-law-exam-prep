### Extending your FSMS to address: — HACCP, TACCP and VACCP

- TACCP and VACCP address threats and vulnerabilities instead of hazards.

- TACCP stands for Threat Assessment and Critical Control Points. This protocol focuses on tampering, intentional adulteration of food, and food defence.

- VACCP stands for Vulnerability Assessment and Critical Control Points. It focuses on food fraud as well, and widens the scope to include systematic prevention of any potential adulteration of food, whether intentional or not, by identifying the vulnerable points in a supply chain. It is especially concerned with economically motivated adulteration (EMA).

### Audits

Through its audit activities the FSAI provides an independent evaluation on the
effectiveness of the Irish official control system. Official control tasks are generally
conducted to verify that food business operators carry out activities (e.g. production,
distribution, retail etc.) in compliance with the requirements of food law.

Following FSAI audits, recommendations for improvement, where necessary, are made in
areas such as:
- environmental health
- veterinary public health
- seafood safety
- food business operator compliance with food law

From time to time, we investigate if the highest standards of food safety and legal
compliance are being applied by all sectors in the food chain. Investigations may be
initiated as a result of food safety incidents, emerging risks or where food fraud is
suspected.

### Cross Contamination

- **What do we mean by cross contamination?**
- It is the process by which a ‘gluten-free’ product loses that status because it comes into contact with something that is not ‘gluten-free’
- ‘Gluten-free’ status must prevail at **every link of the chain**. Cross-contamination with gluten can occur at any stage, unintentionally, for example:

1. Incorrect formulation
2. Inappropriate production sequencing
3. Poor rework
4. Product carry-over
5. Use of common equipment
6. Poor clean-up and/or sanitation
7. Poor equipment design
8. Human error

### “Unintentional presence” Cross contamination & Allergens

- The legislation does not apply to unintentional cross-contamination of foods with allergenic ingredients which are used on a manufacturing site for foods which are known to contain allergens.

- Manufacturers should have in place good manufacturing practices

contain’ statement should not be used as a substitute for GMP.

### Regulation on Deforestation-free Products

EU rules to guarantee that the products EU citizens consume do not contribute to
deforestation or forest degradation worldwide.

The main driver of deforestation is the expansion of agricultural land linked to the
production of commodities like cattle, wood, cocoa, soy, palm oil, coffee, rubber, and
some of their derived products, such as leather, chocolate, tyres, or furniture

Under the Regulation, any operator or trader who places these commodities on the EU
market, or exports from it, must be able to prove that the products do not originate from
recently deforested land or have contributed to forest degradation.

In December 2024 the European Union granted a 12-month additional phasing-in period,
making the law applicable on 30 December 2025 for large and medium companies and
30 June 2026 for micro and small enterprises.

Ref:  Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 and Regulation (EU) 2024/3234

### What is Green Washing?  Is it misleading? — (Definitions:  KPMG Report, 2024)

ESG reporting does not yet require that same standard of rigor as financial reporting at the moment. As a result, a discrepancy
can emerge between what a company claims it is doing and what it is actually doing.

But newer terms to the vernacular such as “greenhushing” and “greenwishing” are taking hold. Here’s a quick rundown:

1.Greenwashing is a practice used by businesses to represent themselves as more sustainable than they truly are. Whether it’s
providing misleading information regarding a product’s sustainability or labeling a fund as “green” when it is not, greenwashing
erodes trust and can have significant repercussions. Importantly, greenwashing is not a static concept – it occurs on a
spectrum, ranging from outright deceit to wishful thinking.

2.Greenhushing refers to a company’s refusal to publicize ESG information. The company may fear pushback from

stakeholders who would find its sustainability efforts lacking or from investors who believe ESG undermines returns. On the
surface, greenhushing is not overtly dishonest; however, it limits the quantity and quality of publicly available information.
Without this transparency, it becomes challenging to analyze corporate climate targets, share best practices on
decarbonization and calculate Scope 3 emissions, which by definition require widespread reporting.

3.Greenwishing, or unintentional greenwashing, describes a practice where a company hopes to meet certain sustainability

commitments but simply does not have the wherewithal to do so. Driven by the pressure to set ambitious sustainability goals,
companies can find themselves committing to targets that they cannot realistically achieve, perhaps because of financial,
technological or organizational constraints. Failing to achieve these targets can undermine trust in these companies and in the
broader system.

The Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) officially entered into force on

## Q1. Plant-based terms: milk, sausage, steak and burger

**Topic area:** Misleading information + Voluntary terms
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Critically discuss whether plant-based products should be allowed to use terms traditionally associated with animal products, such as "milk", "sausage", "steak" and "burger". Debate the issue from the perspective of the meat/dairy lobby and vegetarian or vegan advocates.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Frame the issue as a conflict between consumer protection, legal protection of reserved terms, sustainability and market innovation.
2. **Main body - meat/dairy lobby perspective:**
   - Reg (EU) No 1169/2011 Article 7 prohibits misleading information as to nature, identity, composition and method of production.
   - Reg (EU) No 1308/2013 Article 78 and Annex VII Part III reserve dairy terms such as milk, butter, cheese and yoghurt for dairy products.
   - CJEU Case C-422/16 *TofuTown* confirmed that terms such as soya milk and vegan cheese cannot generally be used for non-dairy products unless an exception applies.
   - Plant-based alternatives may not be nutritionally equivalent to dairy/meat products, so familiar names could imply equivalence.
3. **Main body - vegetarian/vegan advocate perspective:**
   - Consumers understand terms such as "plant-based burger" or "vegan sausage" as format descriptors, not claims that the product contains meat.
   - EU law clearly protects dairy terms, but does not impose an equivalent EU-wide ban on meat-format terms such as burger or sausage.
   - Sustainability and Farm to Fork policy support innovation and informed consumer choice.
   - Article 7 can control genuinely misleading presentation without a blanket prohibition.
4. **Application:** Use the lecture's survey point: some consumers object to meat-style naming, while many vegetarian consumers prefer familiar formats.
5. **Conclusion:** Current EU law is strict for dairy terms and more open for meat-format terms. The best answer distinguishes the legal position from the policy debate.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 7
- Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013, Article 78 and Annex VII Part III
- CJEU Case C-422/16 *TofuTown*
- Farm to Fork Strategy

## Q2. Is the General Food Law fit for purpose?

**Topic area:** General Food Law + Traceability & Risk Analysis
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> "Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 remains fundamentally fit for purpose, but only for the problems it was originally designed to solve." Critically discuss with reference to food crises, EFSA, RASFF, traceability and recall.

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Reg 178/2002 was enacted on 28 January 2002 after BSE, dioxin and wider loss of trust in EU food governance. State whether you think it remains fit for purpose.
2. **Main body - strengths:**
   - Article 5 sets general food-law objectives, including a high level of health and consumer protection.
   - Article 6 embeds risk analysis; Article 7 creates the precautionary principle.
   - Article 14 prohibits unsafe food and defines injurious/unfit food.
   - Article 18 establishes traceability; Article 19 creates withdrawal/recall duties.
   - Article 22 establishes EFSA as independent risk assessor and risk communicator.
   - RASFF supports rapid cross-border notification, e.g. Fenugreek seeds E. coli 2011.
3. **Main body - weaknesses:**
   - Horsemeat scandal 2013 exposed food fraud and authenticity gaps more than classic safety gaps.
   - Complex global supply chains strain one-step-back and one-step-forward traceability.
   - Sustainability, nutrition and greenwashing are not fully solved by a food-safety framework designed after BSE/dioxin.
   - Glyphosate/Roundup debates show continuing tension between risk assessment, risk management and public trust.
4. **Conclusion:** Reg 178/2002 is still the constitutional base of EU food law, but it needs support from stronger fraud, sustainability, labelling and enforcement frameworks.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, Articles 5, 6, 7, 14, 18, 19 and 22
- BSE; Dioxin crisis; Horsemeat scandal; Fenugreek seeds E. coli
- RASFF
- EFSA

## Q3. Greenwashing and sustainability claims

**Topic area:** Misleading information + Voluntary/Sustainability claims
**Difficulty:** debate
**Time budget:** 1 hour

### Question prompt

> Critically discuss how food law controls greenwashing and sustainability claims on food labels. What is currently allowed, what is prohibited, and where are the gaps in the legal framework?

### Answer outline

1. **Opening:** Define greenwashing as environmental or sustainability information that gives a misleading impression about a food's characteristics, production method or overall impact.
2. **Main body - current controls:**
   - Reg 1169/2011 Article 7 prohibits misleading information, including claims about characteristics, method of production and special characteristics shared by all comparable foods.
   - Reg 1169/2011 Article 36 controls voluntary food information: it must not mislead, must not be ambiguous or confusing and, where appropriate, must be based on relevant scientific data.
   - Reg 178/2002 Article 8 protects consumers' interests against fraudulent, deceptive or misleading practices.
   - Specific regulated terms such as organic are controlled by Reg 2018/848, so they cannot be used casually.
3. **Main body - gaps:**
   - Broad sustainability claims such as "eco-friendly", "climate positive" or "sustainable" may lack harmonised food-specific criteria.
   - Private schemes and logos can create information overload.
   - Environmental performance may involve complex lifecycle evidence beyond normal label comprehension.
   - Farm to Fork Strategy signals policy movement toward stronger sustainability labelling.
4. **Application:** Compare "organic" (regulated and certified) with "planet friendly" (potentially vague unless substantiated). Mention gluten-free jam as an analogy for implying special characteristics all similar foods possess.
5. **Conclusion:** Current law can catch obviously misleading claims, but clearer harmonised sustainability standards would improve consumer trust and enforcement.

### Key references to score extra marks
- Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Articles 7 and 36
- Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, Article 8
- Regulation (EU) 2018/848
- Farm to Fork Strategy
